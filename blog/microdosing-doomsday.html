Microdosing doomsday

Wenjun Cao · 4 November 2026

Epistemic status: I have read the recent major debates.[1][2][3][4] I am not taking a strong position of doing nothing, or accelerating, or stopping immediately; I want to look at this in a more structural way.

My view is that these recent major debates are all highly focused on a single point: the moment when the catastrophe finally happens. I call this mindset a one-way-time mindset. The structure of this mindset is this: the coming of a future doomsday is believed to sweep away everything, which then makes everything else seem not very important compared with it. This belief changes the weight given to things.

I want to make two points. First, I want to respond to the idea, which comes up in these debates, that we need to stop before things end.[2][4] My view is: anything is easiest to stop before it has started; as things become more certain, they speed up and become harder to stop. For example, when things like neural networks were still uncertain, only a few people with conviction were trying, but even they were not completely sure. Then, after a series of key nodes, such as AlexNet, the Transformer, and ChatGPT, things have become more validated and more certain. So if the doomsday node, or rather the superintelligence node, really exists, then the closer we get to it, the more humanity, because of all kinds of interests or fundamentally different values, will only go faster and become harder to stop. What you can block is only one path, but there will be countless paths all leading to that place. We have already crossed many key nodes, like the ones I just mentioned, and each key node is already irreversible as far as technology diffusion is concerned; our lives have already been changed.

Second, this single-point view makes people feel that a few key nodes and a few figures can stop it. This mindset can give a sense of control: because if a small number of nodes can reverse or pause this thing, then it seems there is still a definite direction for effort, and so, following that one-way-time mindset, one can align one's life toward that direction, to strive and to try to grasp something. It comes with a yes-or-no mindset: the priority of this thing is placed ahead of the priority of everything else. This is inherently a mutually reinforcing process. This mindset leads to the microdose that Kulveit talks about.[1] Except that this dose is caused not only inside companies, but also by this whole single-point-doomsday worldview.

Through these points, I want to oppose this AI-doomsday-oriented, all-or-nothing mode of thinking, and to shift the focus to irreversible technology diffusion.[5] I think we should, to some extent, move from the one-way-time parallel world inside the microdose into the social and political life that is already happening, to make a comprehensive response to this new kind of thing that has never existed before, rather than only making adjustments, for example by collecting some taxes or handing out some subsidies.[6][7]
